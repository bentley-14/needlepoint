# Mackby — Incident-To / Supervisory-Billing Falsity

## Holding

*United States v. Mackby*, 261 F.3d 821 (9th Cir. 2001), is the leading circuit-level
FCA opinion addressing "incident to" billing falsity. Medicare's incident-to rule allows
a physician to bill for services personally rendered by auxiliary personnel (physical
therapists, medical assistants, etc.) under the supervising physician's own NPI, but only
when specific conditions are satisfied — critically, the physician's presence and
availability for direct supervision (not necessarily in the same room, but in the office
suite and immediately available) and, depending on the arrangement, appropriate
licensure/qualification of the auxiliary staff. In *Mackby*, an unlicensed physical
therapist's services were billed as if properly physician-supervised when they were not.
The Ninth Circuit held this supports both FCA falsity (the claim misrepresents that
incident-to conditions were met) and scienter (the defendant knew or should have known
the supervisory requirement was not satisfied).

## Our application

Mackby is the doctrinal template for any count alleging services were billed under a
physician's NPI without the required physician involvement — a fact pattern common to
allegations of unsupervised mid-level practice, absent/traveling physicians whose NPI
continues to generate claims, or unlicensed staff performing services billed as
physician-rendered.

**What makes this a confirmed-fact anchor (not magnitude-only):** the count needs an
AFFIRMATIVE finding that the supervisory/licensure condition was unmet — not an inference
from high volume. Qualifying evidence includes: staffing schedules or PECOS reassignment
records showing no qualified supervising physician present during the billed period;
documented physician absence (travel, leave, disciplinary suspension, non-credentialing
at the service location) overlapping the claim dates; or licensure-board confirmation
that the auxiliary staff member lacked the required credential. A high volume of
incident-to-billed services, without one of these affirmative findings, is a lead for
further investigation, not a pled Mackby count.

**Damages consequence:** because incident-to eligibility is a condition of billing under
the physician's NPI at all, a confirmed incident-to violation taints the FULL claim, not
merely the differential between physician and non-physician reimbursement rates — apply
the Rogan full-claim framework (see the Rogan/Bornstein damages card) and do not concede
the rate-differential framing, which is the defense's argument, not the floor.

## Common rebuttals / defense playbook

See frontmatter. All three center on requiring affirmative proof (supervision presence,
licensure, or damages theory) rather than accepting an inference from volume or
conceding a differential-damages framing where the violation is categorical.
